Part 1
Cookies & tracking pixels

First, what's a cookie?

A cookie is a small note a website saves in your browser so it remembers you.

Helpful cookies

🛒 Remember you

Keep you logged in, keep items in your cart, and remember your language.

Tracking cookies

👀 Follow you

Follow what you do so a company can show you ads or measure results, including companies other than the site you're on.

What's a cookie banner?

It's the pop-up you see on almost every website, usually at the bottom of the screen:

We use cookies to improve your experience. Accept Decline Settings

It started with phone calls.

In 1967, California passed the California Invasion of Privacy Act (CIPA) to stop people from secretly tapping phone calls. Someone could literally clip onto the line and listen. The rule: you can't listen in on or record a conversation unless everyone on it agrees.

That's why calls start with "this call may be recorded." That announcement is how they get your consent.

Who has to agree to a recording?
New York (one-party consent)Just one person on the call. That can be you.
California (all-party consent)Everyone on the call.

That stricter California rule is why most of these lawsuits are filed there.

Wiretapping

📞 What was said

Listening to the actual conversation.

"Pen register"

🔢 Who called whom

An old device that recorded which numbers someone dialed and when, without the conversation.

Now replace the phone call with your website.

The conversationSomeone visits your site
What they "say"Clicks, scrolls, searches, types
On the lineMeta Pixel, Google Analytics, chat & recording tools
The problemThey never agreed to it

Lawyers argue that a tracking tool from another company, recording what your visitor does without permission, is a third party listening in. That's the wiretapping argument. Collecting their IP address and device info gets compared to the pen register.

👉 It's about where your visitor is, not where your business is. Your business can be in New York. A Californian visiting your site is enough.

"But I have a cookie banner."

So did a lot of the businesses that got sued. A banner is a promise. It doesn't block anything on its own.

  • The millisecond problem: the pixel fires the instant the page loads, before anyone has even seen the banner.
  • "Decline" does nothing: the banner isn't connected to the tracking, so it keeps running after someone says no.
  • No banner at all.
So what is a Meta Pixel?

Yes, it's the one that retargets you.

A Meta Pixel is a small piece of Meta's code you add to your site. When someone visits, it reports back to Meta: "this person viewed the pricing page," "this person signed up," "this person bought." It's why you look at something and then see an ad for it on Instagram.

Why you want one as a business owner

Retargeting

🎯 Bring them back

Show ads to people who visited but didn't buy. They're your warmest audience.

Tracking results

📊 Know what's working

See which ad led to a sign-up or sale, so you stop paying for the ones that don't.

Lookalikes

👯 Find more buyers

Meta studies the people who bought from you and finds more people like them.

Without it

🙈 Ads run blind

If you plan to run Meta ads, you'll want it.

Build a lookalike audience from your buyers

  1. Go to Meta Ads Manager (this covers Facebook and Instagram ads).
  2. Audiences → Create Audience → Custom Audience → Website.
  3. Choose the Purchase event, not just "all visitors." Your pixel has to be set up to report purchases.
  4. Create a Lookalike Audience from it. You'll need about 100 people to start.
  5. No pixel yet? Upload your buyer email list as a Customer List and build the lookalike from that.
Keep the pixel. Just turn it on after they say yes.
The fix

It's a build, not just a banner.

Your AI has to change how your site loads tracking. Here's what that build looks like:

  1. Take the pixel out of the page. Right now it probably loads for everyone, automatically.
  2. Build a real consent banner that remembers the visitor's choice.
  3. Load the pixel only after they click Accept. If they decline, it never loads.
  4. Add a "Cookie settings" link in the footer so people can change their mind.
  5. Do the same for Google Analytics, TikTok, Hotjar, and chat widgets.

How does the banner remember the choice?

It's connected to your browser. Not your Wi-Fi, and not your IP address (your device's return address on the internet). Your actual browser.

Each browser keeps its own separate storage: your passwords, your history, your logins, and your cookie choices. Chrome on your laptop and Safari on your phone don't share any of it.

When you click Accept, the site saves a small note in that browser, something like consent = accepted. Every page checks that note first. Accepted? The pixel loads. Declined or no note? It doesn't.

If you...Does the banner pop up again?
Take your laptop to a café (same browser, new Wi-Fi)No. Your browser remembers.
Switch from Chrome to SafariYes. Different browser, nothing saved.
Open the site on your phoneYes. Different device, different browser.
Open a private / incognito windowYes. Private windows start with nothing saved.
Clear your cookiesYes. The note was erased.

Part 2
Accessibility

Can everyone actually use your app?

Businesses get sued under the ADA (the Americans with Disabilities Act) thousands of times a year when people with disabilities can't use their website. Think of someone who is blind using a screen reader that reads the page out loud, or someone who can't use a mouse.

What gets checked

Images

🖼️ Alt text

A short description of each image, so a screen reader can say what's there. (Example below.)

Keyboard

⌨️ No mouse needed

Can you reach every button and link using only the Tab key?

Color

🎨 Contrast

Is the text dark or light enough against its background to read?

Forms

📝 Labels

Every field says what it's for, not just a gray hint that disappears.

What alt text sounds like

Alt text is a short written description hidden in your site's code. A screen reader (software that reads the page out loud for people who are blind or have low vision) reads it aloud. Picture a photo of you teaching on your laptop:

Alt textWhat the screen reader says
❌ None"Image. IMG_4382.jpg"
❌ Too vague"Photo"
✅ Good"Dr. Erin Jacques teaching a live class on her laptop"

Rule of thumb: describe what someone would miss if they couldn't see it. Purely decorative images should be skipped so the screen reader doesn't read out clutter.

"But I have the accessibility button."

You've seen this little figure in the corner of websites. Click it and a menu opens: bigger text, more contrast, and so on. It adds a menu on top of your site. It doesn't fix the missing alt text, keyboard problems, or labels underneath.

👉 Those accessibility widgets don't protect you. In 2025 the FTC fined accessiBe $1M for claiming its widget made sites compliant.

Your accessibility statement

A short page linked in your footer that says:

  • You're committed to making your app usable for everyone.
  • The standard you're aiming for: WCAG 2.2 AA.
  • Any known issues you're still working on.
  • How to reach you if something doesn't work for them.
  • The date it was last updated.

Part 3
Row Level Security (RLS)

Who can see the data in your app?

Your Supabase database is like one big filing cabinet holding every user's information: names, emails, profiles, what they paid for. Each user's information is a row.

Here's the part most people don't know: your website has a public key in its code so it can talk to that cabinet. Anyone can find that key. That's normal and by design, as long as the cabinet has locks.

Without RLS

🔓 No locks

Anyone with your public key could read, change, or delete every user's row.

With RLS

🔐 A lock on every drawer

Each user can only open their own rows. Everyone else gets nothing.

What RLS prevents

  • Data leaks: a stranger downloading your whole list of users and their emails.
  • Snooping: one logged-in user seeing another user's information.
  • Damage: someone changing or deleting records that aren't theirs.
