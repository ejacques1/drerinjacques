## 5:30 — THE RICH TEST

Remember RICH? This is where it becomes useful.

When you find a problem inside an industry, run it through four questions.

**[EDITOR: Build RICH one letter at a time.]**

### R — REPETITIVE

Does someone do this again, and again, and again?

If it happens once a year, that's not where I'd start. Thirty times a day? Different story.

Look back at the examples. Appointments happen every day. Research happens constantly. Contracts keep coming.

Repetition creates volume. And volume creates the opportunity.

### I — IRRITATING

Does somebody hate doing it?

This matters. People don't pay because technology is impressive. **They pay to make problems disappear.**

Nobody is saying, *"I wish I could spend more time searching hundred-page contracts."*

### C — COSTLY

Does the problem have money attached?

That might be employee hours. Lost customers. Missed leads. Or an expensive professional spending hours on work that doesn't require their expertise.

This is where a frustrating problem becomes a valuable one.

### H — HAS A CLEAR OUTCOME

Can you tell whether it actually worked?

Was the appointment booked? Was the document processed?

The clearer the outcome, the easier it is to define what the AI is responsible for.

**[EDITOR: Completed RICH graphic.]**

Repetitive. Irritating. Costly. Has a clear outcome.

When all four show up together — **pay attention.**

But a RICH problem is not automatically a business. There's one more question.

## 8:05 — FIND IT IN YOUR JOB

Now do this with me.

Write down the industry you know best. Not the one that sounds exciting — the one you actually understand. Teacher. Accountant. Nurse. Realtor. Whatever it is.

That's your RICH test.

Now the question that turns an automation into a business: **who writes the check if I make this disappear?**

## 9:05 — GO NARROWER

*"Teachers spend too much time on admin."* Too broad. **Which admin?**

*"Realtors need help with leads."* Too broad. **Where exactly are the leads being lost?**

Go narrower. Narrow problems are easier to understand, easier to test, easier to explain, and easier to sell.

McKinsey looked at the economics of agentic workflows this year, and found that running them is genuinely expensive — a single customer-facing agent workflow at a bank can cost twenty to thirty thousand dollars to run. Multi-agent teams, far more.

**[EDITOR: Show McKinsey source.]**

Which means the value has to clear the cost.

So don't build an AI employee that does forty-seven things. **Solve one expensive problem extremely well.**

## 9:45 — YOUR EXPERIENCE IS THE DATA

If you've worked inside an industry for five, ten, twenty years, you've collected something genuinely valuable. **Domain knowledge.**

Here's what I mean.

Which is why *"What AI business should I start?"* is the wrong question.
