Part 1
Fake testimonials

"Sarah J., Marketing Director ★★★★★"

Ask AI to build a landing page and it will happily fill the testimonial section with glowing reviews from people who don't exist. Placeholder names, five stars, a stock photo. A lot of people launch without ever deleting them.

Oct 2024
The FTC's rule banning fake reviews and testimonials took effect, including AI-generated ones

What the rule bans

  • Reviews from people who don't exist, including ones AI wrote.
  • Reviews from people who never used your product or service.
  • Buying reviews, or paying for only positive ones.
  • Fake numbers: made-up customer counts, star ratings, or follower counts.

Check A · Google Fonts

Your font is phoning Google.

Most AI-built sites get their fonts from Google with one line of code, hidden in the page:

<link href="https://fonts.googleapis.com/css2?family=DM+Sans" rel="stylesheet">

That line tells every visitor's browser: "Go to Google and get this font."

What it looks like (from my own site, which I'm fixing)

When someone opens the page, their browser quietly makes two trips to Google before they've clicked anything:

RequestWhat it is
fonts.googleapis.com/css2?family=DM+Sans…Asks Google for the font instructions
fonts.gstatic.com/s/dmsans/…woff2Downloads the font file from Google

What Google receives with each trip

  • The visitor's IP address (their device's return address on the internet, which can reveal roughly where they are)
  • Their browser and device type
  • Which website they're on
  • When they visited

Why that's a problem

Your visitor never agreed to share any of that with Google. In 2022, a German court ruled a website broke Europe's privacy law by doing exactly this, and ordered it to pay the visitor €100. That started a wave of demand letters across Europe. It's the same issue as the pixel: a third party gets your visitor's data without asking.

What about the US?

  • No US ruling on Google Fonts yet.
  • But IP addresses do count. California's privacy law lists an IP address as personal information, and so do the privacy laws many other states have passed.
  • Lawyers have sued over it. Under California's CIPA (the California Invasion of Privacy Act), lawyers have sued businesses, arguing that tools quietly collecting visitors' IP addresses are a problem. Courts are split: some threw those cases out, and one in late 2025 let a case move forward.

See it yourself

  1. Open your site, right-click, choose Inspect, and open the Network tab.
  2. Type fonts.g in the filter box, then reload.
  3. See fonts.googleapis.com or fonts.gstatic.com? Your site is doing this.

The fix: host the font yourself

Download the font and serve it from your own site. Same look, no trip to Google, and it often loads faster.

Check B · Row Level Security

How a stranger reads your database.

Supabase has Row Level Security (RLS), but it's a switch, and it isn't on for every table. Tables made in Supabase's Table Editor usually have it on. Tables your AI creates with code often don't.

How someone gets in

  1. Every Supabase project gets a web address for each table. Ask that address for the "users" table and it answers.
  2. Your site's code contains your project address and public key, so your app can talk to the database. Anyone can find them: right-click, Inspect, Network tab.
  3. With RLS off, a stranger pastes that address and key into a simple request and gets every row: names, emails, phone numbers. They can also add, change, or delete rows.
  4. With RLS on, the same request comes back empty, unless a rule allows it, like "users can only see their own rows."
Without RLS

🔓 Everything

The stranger's request returns every user's information.

With RLS

🔐 Nothing

The same request returns an empty list, and adding fake data is blocked.

👉 I tested this on my own testimonial system. Using only my site's public key, reading the testimonials came back empty, and trying to add a fake testimonial was blocked: "new row violates row-level security policy."

Check C · Rate limiting

Stop bots without locking out people.

Rate limiting is a cap on how many times someone can try something in a set amount of time, like 5 login attempts per minute.

Why you need it

  • Password guessing: bots try thousands of password combinations on your login page.
  • Spam: bots flood your forms with junk submissions.
  • Your bill: bots hammer your AI features and run up your costs.

Supabase limits some things automatically, like login emails. Your own forms and AI features need their own limit. My testimonial form has one.

Nobody has to wait hours

You choose the rules. The common options:

OptionHow it works
Short window"5 tries per minute." After that, wait 60 seconds. Most people never notice.
Gradual slowdownEach failed try adds a longer wait: 1 second, then 5, then 30. People barely feel it. Bots slow to a crawl.
Prove you're humanAfter a few failed tries, show a "Verify you're human" checkbox instead of blocking.
Reset by emailOffer "Send me a login link" so a real person is never stuck.

Limits reset on their own once the time window passes. Nothing to do manually.

Check D · Consent records

Save a record of every cookie choice.

Last week: your cookie banner asks before tracking anyone. This week: keep proof of the answer.

Why it matters

  • In Europe, privacy law (GDPR) requires you to be able to prove someone agreed.
  • In the US, there isn't a strict rule for small sites, but if someone ever claims your pixel tracked them without permission, this record is your proof.

Where it's kept

In your database (Supabase), in its own table. One row per choice:

What's savedExample
A random ID for that browsera7f3-29c1
What they choseAccepted
WhenOct 16, 2026, 2:14 PM
Which banner version they sawv2

How the random ID protects you

That same random ID is also saved in the visitor's browser cookie. If anyone claims they were tracked without permission, you can match the ID in their browser to your record showing that browser clicked Accept on a specific date, without ever storing who they are.

Why not save their name or IP address?

Your record has to prove a choice was made, not who made it. An IP address counts as personal data, so storing it means more to protect, more to disclose in your privacy policy, and more to delete if someone asks. If your database ever leaked, a list of random IDs exposes nobody.

  • Logged-in users? Attach the record to their account ID instead.
